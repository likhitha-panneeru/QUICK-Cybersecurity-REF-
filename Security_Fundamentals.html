Module 6 — core cybersecurity concepts, security principles, authentication, cryptography, malware, social engineering, frameworks and risk management.

6.1 Cybersecurity Basics

Cybersecurity: The practice of protecting systems, networks, programs and data from digital attacks, unauthorized access, damage and theft. It encompasses technology, processes and people, with the goal of reducing cyber risk and preventing unauthorized exploitation.
Information Security (InfoSec): The broader discipline of protecting information in all forms—digital and physical—from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification or destruction. Cybersecurity is a subset of InfoSec focused specifically on digital/cyber threats; InfoSec also covers paper records, physical documents and verbal communications.
Information Assurance: Ensures that information and information systems are available, reliable and secure when needed. Its five commonly stated pillars are availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality and non-repudiation. It is especially associated with government and military contexts and includes risk management, governance and compliance concerns.

Cyber Threat
Any potential danger that can exploit a vulnerability and cause harm. Threats can include malicious actors, zero-days, human error, natural disasters and system failures.
Attack
A deliberate action that exploits a weakness. Active attacks alter or disrupt; passive attacks observe or eavesdrop.
Vulnerability
A weakness in software, configuration, design, process or people that can be exploited. Examples include buffer overflows, SQL injection, default passwords and missing encryption.
Risk
The possibility and consequence of a threat exploiting a vulnerability. A useful conceptual model is Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact.
Exploit
A technique or code that takes advantage of a vulnerability to obtain unauthorized access or cause harm.
Payload
The malicious action performed after exploitation, such as a reverse shell, ransomware encryption, keylogging or data theft.
Mitigation
An action that reduces likelihood, severity or impact, such as patching, MFA, firewall rules or user training.
Control
A safeguard protecting confidentiality, integrity or availability. Controls may be administrative, technical or physical.

6.2 CIA Triad

The CIA Triad is the foundational information-security model. Security decisions and controls generally protect one or more of confidentiality, integrity and availability.
Confidentiality
Information is accessible only to authorized parties. Controls: encryption at rest/in transit, access controls, MFA, classification, need-to-know and physical security. Example breach: an attacker steals an unencrypted customer-card database.
Integrity
Data remains accurate, complete and unaltered without authorization. Controls: SHA-256 hashes, digital signatures, checksums, database constraints, access controls and audit logs. Example: changing a $100 transaction to $10,000 destroys integrity.
Availability
Authorized users can access systems/data when needed. Controls: redundancy, failover, DDoS mitigation, backups, UPS/generators and capacity planning. Example: ransomware makes hospital records unavailable.
Trade-offs: Strong confidentiality controls can introduce latency or access friction; maximizing availability can conflict with restrictive access. Security requires a balance based on business and system priorities.
Additional properties: Non-repudiation helps prevent denial of an action, authentication verifies identity, and accountability enables actions to be traced through logs and audit trails.

6.3 Security Principles

Least Privilege: Every user, process and program should receive only the minimum permissions required. A web application might need SELECT access to specific tables, not database administration. Run services as non-root users and avoid unnecessary administrator rights.
Need to Know: Access should be limited to information a person actually needs for their role, even if they technically have broader authority. Least privilege constrains what someone can do; need-to-know constrains what information they should access.

Defense in Depth

Why layering works: No single control is perfect. If a firewall is bypassed, network monitoring can detect activity; if that fails, host controls can block or contain execution; if compromise occurs, data encryption and access controls can limit the outcome. In web security, HTTPS + WAF + validation + parameterized queries + CSP provide multiple independent barriers.
Zero Trust: “Never trust, always verify.” Do not automatically trust users, devices or networks merely because they are inside a perimeter. Verify explicitly, enforce least privilege and assume breach. Common implementation practices include MFA, micro-segmentation, continuous monitoring and identity-based access.
Separation of Duties: Critical tasks should not be fully controlled by one person. Examples include requiring review before production deployment and separating firewall administration from independent audit.
Secure by Design: Build security into architecture from the start: minimize attack surface, use secure defaults, encrypt sensitive data, validate input, handle errors safely and avoid unnecessary complexity.
Fail Secure / Fail Safe: A security mechanism should fail to a secure state. For example, if a firewall fails, blocking traffic may be safer than allowing everything. Physical safety requirements can justify deliberate exceptions.
Attack Surface Reduction: Minimize entry points: close unnecessary ports, disable unused services, remove unused software/accounts and reduce exposed APIs. On Linux, ss -tlnp can help inventory listening services so unnecessary exposure can be reviewed.

6.4 Authentication & Authorization

Identification: Claiming an identity (“I am Alice”). Authentication: Proving that claim. Authorization: Determining what the authenticated identity may do. Accounting/Auditing: Recording what was actually done. Together, Authentication, Authorization and Accounting form the AAA model.

MFA, SSO, RBAC, ABAC

MFA
Uses two or more different factor types: knowledge (password/PIN), possession (token/phone/security key) and inherence (biometric). Two passwords are not two different factors. TOTP apps, FIDO2 hardware keys and push authentication are common; SMS is weaker because of SIM swapping.
SSO
Authenticate once through an identity provider and access multiple applications. SAML is common in enterprise environments; OIDC is common in modern applications. Central compromise has broad consequences, so protect SSO with strong MFA.
RBAC
Permissions are assigned to roles such as admin, analyst, editor and viewer; users receive permissions through their roles. It is easier to manage than individual permissions at scale.
ABAC
Decisions use attributes of user, resource, action and environment—for example department, clearance, classification, time, device or location.
Privileged Accounts: Admin, root and domain-admin accounts can change configurations and access sensitive data, making them high-value targets. Use MFA, separate daily and administrative accounts, PAM, session recording and just-in-time access.
Service Accounts: Non-human accounts used by applications/services. They can be risky because passwords may be long-lived, permissions excessive and activity poorly monitored. Use unique credentials, rotation, least privilege and non-interactive access.

6.5 Cryptography

Cryptography: The science of securing communication and data using mathematical techniques. Encryption protects confidentiality, hashing supports integrity, and digital signatures support authenticity, integrity and non-repudiation.
Plaintext
Original readable data before encryption, also called cleartext.
Ciphertext
Unreadable output after encryption, meaningful only when processed with the correct cryptographic mechanism/key.
Encryption
Transforms plaintext into ciphertext using an algorithm and key to protect confidentiality.
Decryption
Reverses encryption to recover plaintext with the appropriate key.
Key
A cryptographic parameter controlling the transformation. Algorithms can be public; keys must be protected.

Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric encryption uses the same secret key for encryption and decryption. It is fast and suitable for bulk data such as disk encryption, database encryption, VPN traffic and application data after TLS establishes session keys. The key-distribution problem is the main challenge. Algorithms historically include AES, DES, 3DES, Blowfish, RC4 and ChaCha20.

Asymmetric Cryptography

Asymmetric cryptography uses a mathematically related public/private key pair. Public keys can be shared; private keys remain secret. It is slower than symmetric cryptography and is commonly used for key agreement, digital signatures, certificates, email encryption and SSH authentication. Algorithms include RSA, ECC, Diffie-Hellman and ElGamal.

Hashing and Salting

A hash function maps input of any size to a fixed-size digest. It is deterministic, one-way in its intended use and exhibits an avalanche effect: a tiny input change produces a very different digest. Hashing is not encryption. Collisions—different inputs producing the same digest—make weak algorithms unsuitable for security.

Salting: Add a unique random salt to each password before hashing. The salt is stored with the password hash and need not be secret. It prevents identical passwords from producing identical hashes and defeats the usefulness of precomputed rainbow tables across many users.

Digital Signatures, Certificates and PKI

Digital Signature: The sender hashes a message and uses their private key to create a signature over that hash. The recipient verifies the signature using the sender's public key and compares the independently calculated hash. This provides authentication, integrity and non-repudiation.
Digital Certificate: An X.509 document binding a public key to an identity. It includes identity information, public key, issuer, validity period, serial number and CA signature.
PKI: Public Key Infrastructure is the system of policies, procedures and technology for managing certificates and public keys. Components include Certificate Authorities, Registration Authorities, CRL/OCSP revocation mechanisms and trusted certificate stores. A typical trust chain is Root CA → Intermediate CA → Server Certificate. Let's Encrypt is a widely used automated CA.
Private keys: Must remain secret. Protect them with strong passphrases where applicable, secure storage such as HSMs, restrictive file permissions and safe key-management practices. Key exchange establishes a shared secret over an insecure channel; Diffie-Hellman does this mathematically without transmitting the shared secret itself.

Which algorithm?
Data at rest/in transit → AES-256 (where that strength is appropriate). TLS key agreement → ECDHE. Digital signatures → ECDSA or RSA. Password storage → Argon2, bcrypt or scrypt, not raw SHA-256. File integrity → SHA-256. Message authentication → HMAC-SHA256. Avoid MD5/SHA-1 for security, DES/3DES for new encryption, and RSA keys below modern minimum sizes.

6.7 Security Controls

Security Control: A safeguard or countermeasure implemented to protect confidentiality, integrity and availability and reduce risk. Controls are practical implementations of security principles.

AdministrativePolicies, procedures, training and governance.Security policy, acceptable-use policy, incident response plan, awareness training, background checks, risk assessment, change/vendor management.

6.8 Malware

Malware: Malicious software intentionally designed to damage, disrupt, steal data or gain unauthorized access. Security teams study malware for both attack simulation and defensive detection/response.
Virus
Attaches to a legitimate file/program and normally requires user execution. Spreads by infecting other files. Email attachments, downloads and USB drives are common vectors. Example: ILOVEYOU (2000).
Worm
Self-replicates automatically across networks without requiring user interaction and does not need a host program. Exploits vulnerabilities/open services. Example: WannaCry (2017), which exploited SMB vulnerabilities and affected systems worldwide.
Trojan
Disguises malicious functionality as legitimate software. Does not self-replicate like a worm. May install backdoors, steal data or deliver other malware. Types include RATs, banking trojans, droppers and downloaders.
Ransomware
Encrypts files or locks systems and demands payment. Modern campaigns may use double extortion by stealing data and threatening publication. Examples include WannaCry, NotPetya, REvil, LockBit, Conti and BlackCat. Defenses include backups, patching, filtering, MFA and EDR.
Spyware
Secretly monitors and collects information such as browsing data, keystrokes, screenshots and system information. Example: Pegasus spyware has been reported as targeting mobile devices.
Adware
Displays or downloads unwanted advertisements. Some variants track users or act as a delivery vector for more serious threats.
Rootkit
Seeks high privilege and hides files, processes or network activity from normal tools. Types include user-mode and kernel-mode rootkits; detection can require trusted offline analysis and known-good comparisons.
Bootkit
Rootkit-like malware that infects boot components such as MBR/VBR or UEFI and executes before the OS, potentially surviving OS reinstallation. Secure Boot and verified boot technologies help defend.
Keylogger
Records keystrokes, potentially capturing passwords, payment data and messages. Can be software-based or a physical hardware device.
RAT
Remote Access Trojan provides covert remote control such as screen viewing, file access, keyboard/mouse control and potentially camera/microphone access. Examples include DarkComet, njRAT, AsyncRAT and QuasarRAT.
Botnet
A collection of compromised “bots” controlled through command-and-control infrastructure. Uses include DDoS, spam, credential attacks, mining and click fraud. Mirai is a notable IoT botnet example.
Fileless Malware
Operates primarily in memory and abuses legitimate tools such as PowerShell and WMI (“living off the land”). Defenses include script logging, AMSI, EDR behavior detection and memory analysis.
Cryptominer / Cryptojacker
Secretly uses CPU/GPU resources to mine cryptocurrency. Symptoms include high utilization, heat and degraded performance. Browser-based cryptojacking has also existed.

6.9 Social Engineering

Social engineering: Psychological manipulation that attacks people rather than technology, persuading victims to disclose information or perform actions. Even strong technical controls can be undermined when a user voluntarily provides credentials or approves a malicious request.
Phishing
Fraudulent messages impersonating trusted sources to steal credentials, deliver malware or induce actions. Common signals include urgency, authority, fear and suspicious links.
Spear Phishing
Targeted, researched phishing customized to a particular person or organization, making it more believable than mass phishing.
Whaling
Spear phishing aimed at executives or other high-value individuals, often involving financial transfers or sensitive documents.
Smishing
Phishing through SMS/text messages, such as fake delivery or banking alerts containing malicious links.
Vishing
Voice phishing through phone calls that impersonate banks, support teams, IT or government organizations. Voice cloning can make impersonation more convincing.
Pretexting
Inventing a believable scenario and false identity to persuade a target to provide information or access.
Baiting
Offering something tempting, such as an infected USB labeled with interesting data or malicious “free” software, to induce execution.
Tailgating / Piggybacking
Following an authorized person through a restricted physical entrance by exploiting politeness or pretending to have forgotten credentials.
Business Email Compromise
Impersonating or compromising business email to conduct unauthorized transfers or steal information. Common forms include CEO fraud, invoice fraud and compromised-account requests.

6.10 Security Frameworks

Security frameworks: Structured guidelines, standards and best practices used to manage cybersecurity risk and create a common language for security programs.

NIST Cybersecurity Framework

1IDENTIFY
Know assets, business context and risks.
2PROTECT
Implement safeguards.
3DETECT
Identify security events.
4RESPOND
Take action on incidents.
5RECOVER
Restore and improve operations.

NIST CSF is a flexible framework developed by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology and widely adopted across industries. Its functions provide a common structure for managing cybersecurity risk.

NIST SP 800-61
Incident handling guidance. The lifecycle commonly presented is Preparation → Detection and Analysis → Containment, Eradication and Recovery → Post-Incident Activity.
CIS Controls
A prioritized set of 18 safeguards organized into Implementation Groups IG1, IG2 and IG3. Starts with essential cyber hygiene and progresses to more advanced practices.
ISO/IEC 27001
Requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS), including continual improvement and a control framework. Organizations can pursue certification through accredited auditing.
MITRE ATT&CK
Knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques. Used for detection mapping, threat hunting, red-team planning and identifying visibility gaps.
OWASP
Nonprofit focused on application security. Major resources include OWASP Top 10, Testing Guide, ASVS, SAMM, ZAP, Dependency-Check and ModSecurity Core Rule Set.

Cyber Kill Chain

1Reconnaissance
Gather target information.
2Weaponization
Prepare exploit and payload.
3Delivery
Send weapon via email, web, USB, etc.
4Exploitation
Trigger vulnerability.
5Installation
Establish malware/persistence.
6C2
Communicate with attacker.
7Actions on Objectives
Steal, destroy, ransom or otherwise achieve the goal.

The Cyber Kill Chain is Lockheed Martin's seven-stage model. Defenders can break the chain at any stage; earlier disruption is generally preferable, but even late-stage C2 detection can stop objectives from being completed.

6.11 Risk Management

Risk management: The systematic process of identifying, assessing and controlling threats to an organization's assets and operations. Risk cannot normally be eliminated completely; the goal is to reduce it to an acceptable level.

Core Risk Terms

TermMeaningPractical Question
AssetAnything valuable that needs protection: data, servers, applications, IP, reputation, people and facilities.What do we need to protect?
ThreatPotential danger that can harm an asset.What could cause harm?
VulnerabilityWeakness that a threat could exploit.What weaknesses can be exploited?
LikelihoodProbability of a threat exploiting a vulnerability.How probable is exploitation?
ImpactConsequence if exploitation succeeds: financial, operational, legal, reputational or safety harm.How bad would it be?

Risk Formula

A common conceptual formula is Risk = Likelihood × Impact. Some models additionally express risk using threat and vulnerability. A high-likelihood/low-impact issue may be less urgent than a low-likelihood/catastrophic-impact issue; risk scoring helps prioritize treatment.

Risk Assessment Process

1Asset Identification
Inventory what needs protection.
2Threat Identification
Identify what could go wrong.
3Vulnerability Identification
Find exploitable weaknesses.
4Likelihood Assessment
Estimate probability.
5Impact Assessment
Estimate consequences.
6Risk Rating
Combine likelihood and impact.
7Risk Treatment
Choose how to handle it.

Assessments may be qualitative (high/medium/low) or quantitative (probability and financial values). They should be repeated because assets, threats and vulnerabilities change.

Risk Treatment

Mitigation / Reduction
Implement controls that reduce likelihood or impact: patching, MFA, backups and training.
Acceptance
Consciously accept a risk when it is within risk appetite or mitigation cost outweighs expected benefit. Document who approved it and why.
Transfer
Shift some financial/operational consequence to another party, such as through cyber insurance, managed services or contractual liability. The underlying risk still exists.
Avoidance
Eliminate the activity or asset creating the risk—for example, not collecting sensitive data or retiring an unfixable legacy application.
Risk management is continuous: New threats emerge, vulnerabilities are discovered and business changes introduce assets and processes. A risk register records identified risks, ratings and treatment decisions and should be reviewed regularly. Technical controls, processes and tools ultimately serve the goal of managing organizational risk to an acceptable level.
